A Brief History of Modern Emulation

Below is a brief overview of modern retro gaming emulation. Roughly stating how the practice has evolved and changed over the years.

  • Early 1990s
    The first publicly released emulators appear for home computers. Arcade emulation dominates early interest, driven by a desire to play games outside the arcade without paying per-credit. These early projects are primitive, often requiring hand-tuned code for each individual game.
  • 1997 — MAME
    The Multiple Arcade Machine Emulator launches, fundamentally changing the emulation scene. MAME's open, collaborative model, where contributors add support for individual arcade boards. This would become the basis for several open source projects over the coming years.
  • Late 1990s — Console Emulation Arrives
    Emulators for the NES, SNES, Sega Genesis, and Game Boy proliferate rapidly. Projects like Snes9x and VisualBoyAdvance bring 16-bit and handheld gaming to the PC, sparking both widespread enthusiasm and the first major legal debates around ROM distribution. All in all, a legal nightmare.
  • 2000s — The 3D Generation
    Tackling PlayStation and Nintendo 64 hardware proves far more complex than 16-bit systems. ePSXe and Project64 make early inroads, but accuracy suffers. Meanwhile, PCSX2 begins the monumental task of emulating the PlayStation 2, a project that would take over a decade to mature. Dolphin launches in 2003, targeting the then-current GameCube — an audacious goal at the time.
  • 2010s — Accuracy over Compatibility
    A philosophical shift takes hold. Developers like byuu (bsnes) champion cycle-accurate emulation, arguing that getting the hardware exactly right matters more than raw game compatibility. This era produces some of the most technically impressive emulators ever written, and raises the bar for the entire scene. Dolphin reaches near-perfect GameCube and Wii compatibility. PCSX2 matures significantly.
  • 2013 — RetroArch & libretro
    The libretro API and RetroArch frontend unify dozens of emulators under a single interface with a shared controller, shader, and netplay system. For the first time, a non-technical user can run games from twenty different consoles from one application, on everything from a gaming PC to a Raspberry Pi.
  • 2020s — The Modern Era
    Emulation reaches a new frontier. RPCS3 achieves broad PlayStation 3 compatibility despite the Cell processor's notorious complexity. Yuzu and Ryujinx bring Nintendo Switch emulation to desktop PCs while the hardware is still on sale, triggering renewed legal scrutiny from Nintendo. The Steam Deck's launch in 2022 brings Linux-based handheld emulation to a mass audience, and projects like EmuDeck make setup accessible to anyone.
